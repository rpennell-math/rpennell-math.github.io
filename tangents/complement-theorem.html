Subtraction Without Borrowing

The Method of Complements in Any Base

Ryan Pennell · Ivy Tech Community College, Bloomington, Indiana

PDF version LaTeX source
Abstract The method of complements turns subtraction into addition: to compute $x - y$, replace each digit of $y$ by its distance from the largest digit, add one, add the result to $x$, and discard the leading carry. Binary two's complement and the nines' complement of mechanical calculators are the best-known instances. We give a short, self-contained proof that the method works in every base $N \ge 2$, explain the case of a negative difference, and record the modular interpretation. We then extend the result to mixed-radix systems, which places clock arithmetic, feet and inches, pounds and ounces, and the subtraction of mixed numbers under a single identity. None of the results are new; the aim is a unified exposition.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 11A63. Keywords: method of complements, radix complement, two's complement, positional numeration, mixed radix.

1. Introduction

Ordinary subtraction requires borrowing, and borrowing is where most errors occur, whether the subtractor is a student or a machine. The method of complements avoids it. In base ten, to subtract $187$ from $523$, one replaces each digit of $187$ by its difference from $9$, obtaining $812$; adds one to get $813$; and computes $523 + 813 = 1336$. Dropping the leading $1$ leaves $336$, which is the correct difference.

The method is old. Pascal's calculating machine displayed the nines' complement of its result alongside the result itself so that subtraction could be performed by addition, and the keys of the Comptometer carried complement digits in small type for the same purpose [2], [4]. It has also been taught to prospective elementary school teachers [1]. In modern computing, the binary version, two's complement, is the standard representation of negative integers [3].

This note proves the method in an arbitrary base (Section 2), interprets it modularly, and generalizes it to mixed radix (Section 3), where it explains several familiar “regrouping” computations at once.

2. The Method in Base $N$

Fix a base $N \ge 2$ and a working length of $k \ge 1$ digits. Every integer $y$ with $0 \le y < N^k$ has a unique representation \[ y = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} d_i N^i, \qquad 0 \le d_i \le N-1, \] padded with leading zeros as needed.

Definition 2.1. The digit complement (or diminished radix complement) of $y$ is \[ \dc{y} = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} (N-1-d_i)\, N^i . \] The radix complement of $y$ is $\rc{y} = \dc{y} + 1$.

In base $2$ these are the one's and two's complements, and in base $10$ they are the nines' and tens' complements.

Lemma 2.2. For $0 \le y < N^k$, we have $\dc{y} = N^k - 1 - y$. Consequently $\rc{y} = N^k - y$.
Proof. The $k$-digit numeral whose digits all equal $N-1$ has value \[ \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} (N-1)N^i = N^k - 1 \] by the geometric series. Because each $d_i \le N-1$, the subtraction $(N^k-1) - y$ can be performed column by column with no borrowing, and column $i$ yields the digit $N-1-d_i$. Hence $(N^k - 1) - y = \dc{y}$, and adding $1$ gives the second statement. $\square$

The absence of borrowing in this proof is the whole point of the method: the only subtraction it performs is digit by digit against the largest digit, which never fails.

Theorem 2.3 (Method of complements). Let $N \ge 2$ and $k \ge 1$. If $0 \le y \le x < N^k$, then \[ x + \rc{y} = N^k + (x - y). \] In particular, the base-$N$ numeral for $x + \rc{y}$ consists of a $1$ in the $N^k$ place followed by the $k$ digits of $x - y$, so discarding the leading $1$ yields $x - y$.
Proof. By Lemma 2.2, $x + \rc{y} = x + (N^k - y) = N^k + (x - y)$. Since $0 \le x - y < N^k$, the numeral for $N^k + (x - y)$ is a $1$ followed by the $k$-digit numeral for $x - y$. $\square$

Remark 2.4 (Negative differences). If instead $0 \le x < y < N^k$, then \[ x + \rc{y} = N^k - (y - x) = \rc{(y-x)} < N^k . \] No carry leaves the $k$-th place, and the result is the radix complement of the magnitude $y - x$. The presence or absence of a final carry therefore records the sign of the difference. This is the principle behind signed two's-complement arithmetic in computers.

Remark 2.5 (Modular interpretation). Lemma 2.2 says precisely that $\rc{y} \equiv -y \pmod{N^k}$. The radix complement is the additive inverse of $y$ in $\mathbb{Z}/N^k\mathbb{Z}$, and discarding the leading $1$ is reduction modulo $N^k$.

Example 2.6.
  1. Base $10$. For $523 - 187$: $\dc{187} = 812$, $\rc{187} = 813$, and $523 + 813 = 1336$, giving $336$.
  2. Base $5$. For $431_5 - 124_5$ (that is, $116 - 39$): $\dc{124_5} = 320_5$, $\rc{124_5} = 321_5$, and $431_5 + 321_5 = 1302_5$, giving $302_5 = 77$.
  3. Base $2$. For $1101_2 - 0110_2$ (that is, $13 - 6$): $\dc{0110_2} = 1001_2$, $\rc{0110_2} = 1010_2$, and $1101_2 + 1010_2 = 10111_2$, giving $0111_2 = 7$.

3. Mixed Radix

Nothing in the argument requires every place to share a base. Let $N_0, N_1, \dots, N_{k-1} \ge 2$ be the bases of the successive places, and define place values \[ P_0 = 1, \qquad P_{i+1} = N_i P_i, \qquad M = P_k = N_0 N_1 \cdots N_{k-1}. \] Every integer $0 \le y < M$ has a unique representation $y = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} d_i P_i$ with $0 \le d_i \le N_i - 1$. Define the digit complement by $\dc{y} = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} (N_i - 1 - d_i) P_i$ and the radix complement by $\rc{y} = \dc{y} + 1$.

Theorem 3.1. In the mixed-radix system above, $\dc{y} = M - 1 - y$ and $\rc{y} = M - y$. Consequently, if $0 \le y \le x < M$, then $x + \rc{y} = M + (x - y)$, and discarding the carry into the $M$ place yields $x - y$.
Proof. The numeral whose digits are all maximal has value \[ \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} (N_i - 1)P_i = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} (P_{i+1} - P_i) = P_k - P_0 = M - 1, \] a telescoping sum. As before, subtracting $y$ from it requires no borrowing, which gives $\dc{y} = M - 1 - y$. The remaining statements follow exactly as in Theorem 2.3. $\square$

Taking every $N_i = N$ recovers Lemma 2.2, since then $M = N^k$.

Example 3.2. In each case below, only the last place is complemented, the complement is added, and one unit of the next place is removed.
  1. Minutes (base $60$). To find $20$ minutes before 3:15, add $60 - 20 = 40$ minutes to get 3:55, then remove one hour: 2:55.
  2. Feet and inches (base $12$). For $5\text{ ft }3\text{ in} - 7\text{ in}$, add $12 - 7 = 5$ inches to get $5\text{ ft }8\text{ in}$, then remove a foot: $4\text{ ft }8\text{ in}$.
  3. Pounds and ounces (base $16$). For $6\text{ lb }4\text{ oz} - 9\text{ oz}$, add $16 - 9 = 7$ ounces to get $6\text{ lb }11\text{ oz}$, then remove a pound: $5\text{ lb }11\text{ oz}$.
  4. Mixed numbers. The denominator plays the role of the base. For $5\tfrac14 - \tfrac34$, add $1 - \tfrac34 = \tfrac14$ to get $5\tfrac24$, then remove one whole: $4\tfrac24 = 4\tfrac12$.

Every example above is an instance of the identity \[ a - b = a + (M - b) - M, \] where $M$ is the unit being “borrowed” from. In the classroom this is the familiar strategy of counting up, the same procedure a cashier uses to make change.
