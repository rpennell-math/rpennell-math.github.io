Radix Economy and the Number $e$

Why Base Three Is the Most Economical Integer Base

Ryan Pennell · Department of Mathematics, Ivy Tech Community College, Bloomington, Indiana

PDF version LaTeX source
Abstract If a digit capable of $b$ distinct states is assigned cost $b$, the cost of writing an integer $N$ in base $b$ is the radix economy $E(b,N) = b\,(\lfloor \log_b N\rfloor + 1)$. We give a self-contained treatment of the classical fact that this cost is asymptotically $(b/\ln b)\ln N$, that the function $x/\ln x$ is uniquely minimized on $(1,\infty)$ at $x=e$, and that among integer bases the unique minimizer is $b=3$, a fact that reduces to the inequality $2^3<3^2$. We further show that no mixed-radix system built from integer radices can improve on pure ternary, and that base $e$ itself, while optimal in the continuous model, is unusable for exact integer arithmetic: by the transcendence of $e$, no integer $N\ge 3$ has a finite base-$e$ expansion.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary 11A63; Secondary 26A06, 68R01. Keywords: radix economy, positional numeral systems, ternary, balanced ternary, $\beta$-expansions.

1. Introduction

Why do computers count in base two, and is there a sense in which some other base would be better? A classical answer, going back at least to the early days of electronic computing and popularized in Knuth [3] and Hayes [1], is the notion of radix economy. One models the cost of a digit as proportional to the number of states it must distinguish, and asks which base minimizes the total cost of representing large numbers. The answer is the number $e\approx 2.718$, and the best integer base is therefore $3$. The idea was realized in hardware in the Soviet SETUN computer (Moscow State University, 1958), which used balanced ternary arithmetic [1].

The purpose of this note is to give a complete and rigorous account of this folklore result, together with two complements that are less often stated precisely: an optimality result for mixed-radix systems (Section 5), and an obstruction to using base $e$ directly (Section 6).

2. Setup and the asymptotic cost

Definition 2.1. For an integer base $b\ge 2$ and an integer $N\ge 1$, let \[ d_b(N) = \lfloor \log_b N \rfloor + 1 \] denote the number of base-$b$ digits of $N$. The radix economy of $N$ in base $b$ is \[ E(b,N) = b\, d_b(N). \] Define $f\colon(1,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ by \[ f(x) = \frac{x}{\ln x}. \]

The following lemma shows that, for large $N$, comparing bases by radix economy is the same as comparing the values of $f$.

Lemma 2.2. For each fixed integer $b\ge 2$, \[ \lim_{N\to\infty} \frac{E(b,N)}{\ln N} = f(b). \] Equivalently, $E(b,N) = f(b)\ln N + O(1)$ as $N\to\infty$.
Proof. For every real $y$ we have $y < \lfloor y\rfloor + 1 \le y+1$. Taking $y = \log_b N = \ln N/\ln b$ gives \[ \frac{\ln N}{\ln b} < d_b(N) \le \frac{\ln N}{\ln b} + 1 . \] Multiplying by $b$ yields \[ f(b)\ln N < E(b,N) \le f(b)\ln N + b, \tag{2.1} \] which is the second statement. Dividing by $\ln N$ for $N\ge 2$ gives \[ f(b) < \frac{E(b,N)}{\ln N} \le f(b) + \frac{b}{\ln N}, \] and since $b/\ln N\to 0$, the squeeze theorem gives the limit. $\square$

3. The continuous optimum

Theorem 3.1. The function $f(x)=x/\ln x$ attains a unique global minimum on $(1,\infty)$ at $x=e$, and $f(e)=e$.
Proof. The function $f$ is differentiable on $(1,\infty)$, with \[ f'(x) = \frac{\ln x \cdot 1 - x\cdot \tfrac{1}{x}}{(\ln x)^2} = \frac{\ln x - 1}{(\ln x)^2}. \] For $x>1$ the denominator is positive, so $f'(x)$ has the sign of $\ln x - 1$. Thus $f'(x)<0$ for $1<x<e$ and $f'(x)>0$ for $x>e$. By the Mean Value Theorem, $f$ is strictly decreasing on $(1,e]$ and strictly increasing on $[e,\infty)$. Consequently $f(x)>f(e)$ for every $x\neq e$, and $f(e)=e/\ln e = e$. $\square$

Remark 3.2. There is an information-theoretic reading of Theorem 3.1. A digit with $b$ states carries $\ln b$ nats of information, so $f(b)=b/\ln b$ is the cost per nat. The number $e$ is the state count that delivers the most information per unit cost. The natural logarithm appears because the nat is the natural unit of information; changing the base of the logarithm rescales $f$ by a positive constant and does not move the minimizer.

4. Integer bases

Corollary 4.1. Among integers $b\ge 2$, the value $f(b)$ is uniquely minimized at $b=3$.
Proof. Since $2<e<3$, Theorem 3.1 shows that $f$ is strictly increasing on $[3,\infty)$, so $f(3)<f(4)<f(5)<\cdots$. It remains to compare $b=2$ with $b=3$: \[ f(3)<f(2) \iff \frac{3}{\ln 3} < \frac{2}{\ln 2} \iff 3\ln 2 < 2\ln 3 \iff \ln 8 < \ln 9, \] which holds because $8<9$. $\square$
Corollary 4.2. For each fixed integer $b\ge 2$ with $b\neq 3$, there is an $N_0$ such that \[ E(3,N) < E(b,N) \qquad\text{for all } N\ge N_0 . \]
Proof. By Lemma 2.2, \[ \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{E(b,N)-E(3,N)}{\ln N} = f(b)-f(3) > 0, \] the inequality being Corollary 4.1. Hence the quotient is eventually positive, and since $\ln N>0$ for $N\ge 2$, so is the numerator. $\square$

Remark 4.3. Corollary 4.2 is pointwise in $b$: it does not assert that base $3$ is optimal for every individual $N$. Because of the floor function, small values of $N$ can produce ties or reversals.

5. Mixed radices cannot beat ternary

One might hope to approach the continuous optimum $e$ by mixing integer radices, for instance alternating binary and ternary positions. In a mixed-radix system with positional radices $b_1,\dots,b_k$ (integers $\ge 2$), the number of representable values is $\prod_{i} b_i$, and under the same cost model the total cost is $\sum_i b_i$.

Proposition 5.1. Let $b_1,\dots,b_k\ge 2$ be integers and suppose $\prod_{i=1}^k b_i \ge N$. Then \[ \sum_{i=1}^k b_i \;\ge\; f(3)\,\ln N, \] with equality if and only if $b_i=3$ for every $i$ and $N=3^k$.
Proof. By Corollary 4.1, $f(b_i)\ge f(3)$ for each $i$, that is, $b_i \ge f(3)\ln b_i$. Summing over $i$, \[ \sum_{i=1}^k b_i \;\ge\; f(3)\sum_{i=1}^k \ln b_i = f(3)\ln\prod_{i=1}^k b_i \;\ge\; f(3)\ln N . \] Equality in the first step requires $f(b_i)=f(3)$ for every $i$, which by the uniqueness in Corollary 4.1 forces $b_i=3$. Equality in the last step requires $\prod_i b_i = N$, which is then $N = 3^k$. Conversely, these conditions clearly give equality. $\square$

Thus no arrangement of integer radices, however cleverly mixed, improves on pure ternary in this model. The constant $f(3)=3/\ln 3$ is a universal lower bound for integer-digit positional systems.

6. Base $e$ itself

Positional representations in a non-integer base $\beta>1$ were introduced by Rényi [5] and studied further by Parry [4]. For $\beta=e$, the greedy algorithm expands any real $x\ge 0$ using the digit set $\{0,1,2\}$.

Example 6.1. The greedy base-$e$ expansion of $10$ begins \[ 10 = 1\cdot e^2 + 0\cdot e + 2 + 1\cdot e^{-1} + 1\cdot e^{-2} + 2\cdot e^{-3} + 0\cdot e^{-4} + 1\cdot e^{-5} + \cdots, \] that is, $10 = (102.11201\ldots)_e$.

The expansion in the example does not terminate, and this is unavoidable.

Proposition 6.2. No integer $N\ge 3$ admits a finite expansion \[ N = \sum_{i=-m}^{n} d_i\, e^{i}, \qquad d_i\in\{0,1,2\}. \]
Proof. Suppose such an expansion exists, with $m,n\ge 0$. Multiplying by $e^m$ shows that $e$ is a root of \[ P(x) = \sum_{i=-m}^{n} d_i\, x^{i+m} - N x^m \in \mathbb{Z}[x]. \] The coefficient of $x^m$ in $P$ is $d_0 - N$, which is nonzero because $d_0\le 2<N$. Hence $P$ is a nonzero integer polynomial with $P(e)=0$, contradicting the transcendence of $e$ (Hermite [2]). $\square$

So base $e$ is optimal in the continuous model but cannot represent even small integers exactly with finitely many digits. Base $3$ is the practical realization of the optimum.

7. Remarks on the cost model

The conclusions above depend on the assumption that a $b$-state digit costs an amount proportional to $b$. In digital hardware this assumption fails: two-state devices are cheap and robust against noise, while intermediate voltage levels reduce noise margins. This is a principal reason binary logic prevails despite its roughly $5\%$ theoretical disadvantage. Multi-level encodings do appear where density is paramount, for example in multi-level flash memory cells and four-level pulse-amplitude signaling.

The same optimization governs branching structures. In a tree of branching factor $b$ holding $N$ leaves, where selecting a child costs a linear scan of up to $b$ options, the total search cost is $b\log_b N = f(b)\ln N$, again minimized at $e$ and, over integers, at $3$. If instead a child is selected by binary search at cost $\log_2 b$, the total becomes $\log_2 N$ regardless of $b$, and the optimization disappears. The appearance of $e$ is therefore a consequence of linear cost per state, not a law of nature.
